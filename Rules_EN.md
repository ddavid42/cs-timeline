# How to Play: Timeline of Computing

This game invites players to place historical events, inventions, and discoveries from the history of computing (from the birth of arithmetic 100,000 years ago to the rise of generative AI in 2022) in chronological order. The game is as much about playing as it is about discussing the historical context of each event.

## Materials

Each card has two sides:

- **Front**: the event title (and sometimes a subtitle naming the person or project involved), without the date.
- **Back**: the date, the same title, and an explanatory text.

The deck contains 65 cards covering the history of computing and calculation, from prehistory to the present day.

## Goal of the Game

Be the first player to have no cards left in hand by placing cards correctly in chronological order on the table.

## Setup

1. Shuffle the deck with the fronts visible (titles only, with no dates).
2. Draw one card at random, turn it over to reveal its back (and date), and place it in the middle of the table. This is the first chronological reference point on the timeline.
3. Deal the same number of cards to each player, fronts visible, without looking at the dates:
   - 4 cards for 2 to 4 players;
   - 3 cards for 5 or more players.
4. Place the remaining cards face up in a draw pile in the middle of the table.

## Taking a Turn

On their turn, a player:

1. Chooses a card from their hand. They know only its title, not its date.
2. Places it on the timeline already built, where they think the event belongs: before, after, or between two cards already on the table.
3. Turns the card over to reveal its date:
   - **If the placement is correct** (the date is consistent with the neighboring cards), the card remains on the timeline with its back visible and becomes a new chronological reference point.
   - **If the placement is incorrect**, the card is removed from play and the player draws a new card to refill their hand.
4. The next player takes their turn.

The turn passes even after an incorrect placement; the player does not play again immediately.

## End of the Game and Winning

- The game ends as soon as a player has no cards left in hand.
- That player wins the game.
- Variant: if the draw pile is empty before a player wins, the player with the fewest cards left in hand wins.

## Variants and Facilitation Tips

As with a Climate Fresk workshop, the game benefits from a collective discussion, especially when facilitating sessions with children:

- **Chronological debrief**: once all cards have been placed, including the cards set aside, rebuild the complete timeline and discuss the major periods, key figures, and the role of women in computing.
- **Team play**: play in small groups that must agree together before placing each card. This encourages evidence-based discussion rather than simple memorization.
- **Discovery mode**: allow players to read the explanatory text on the back after every placement, correct or incorrect, to use the game as an educational activity rather than only a competition.
- **Timed mode**: for a shorter activity, set a time limit and count the number of correctly placed cards for each team at the end, rather than waiting for one hand to be empty.
- **Human timeline** (ideal for a class, for example a group of 10 students): give each student one random card without showing its date. Each student must stand in a line, or arrange themselves around a table, where they think their event belongs in relation to the students already in place. Once everyone is positioned, check the human timeline together by turning over the cards one at a time in the order of the line. Students whose cards are out of place move until the chronological order is correct. This variant encourages students to look for clues in the titles, such as styles and technological references, and to discuss their ideas as a group rather than compete individually.
